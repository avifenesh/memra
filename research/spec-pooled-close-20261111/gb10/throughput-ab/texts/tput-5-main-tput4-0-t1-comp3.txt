The lighthouse keeper’s log book was the solitary heartbeat of the station, a solemn record of duty, weather, and isolation. Far from the modern era of digital telemetry, the log was a physical artifact, typically bound in heavy, oil-resistant leather or canvas, designed to withstand the constant humidity and salt spray of coastal life. It was not merely a diary, but a legal document and a technical manual in one, recording events with clinical precision.

Daily, the routine began with the rise of the sun. The keeper would enter the log before attending to the lens cleaning or the maintenance of the light mechanism. The entries followed a rigid structure. First, the weather: barometric pressure, wind direction and velocity, wave height, and visibility. These observations were critical for maritime safety, as sudden squalls could swallow vessels whole. Next, the state of the light.
