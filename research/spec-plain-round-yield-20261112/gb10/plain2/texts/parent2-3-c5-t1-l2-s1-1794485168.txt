# The Comprehensive Manual for Planning, Executing, and Documenting a Mountain Rescue Exercise

## Introduction

Mountain rescue operations are distinct from standard urban emergency services due to the extreme variability of the environment, the logistical challenges of remote access, and the high stakes involved in saving lives in hostile terrain. While the ultimate goal of any rescue service is to save lives during actual emergencies, the effectiveness of this service depends almost entirely on rigorous, repetitive, and highly structured training. A mountain rescue exercise is not merely a role-play; it is a controlled simulation designed to stress-test protocols, test equipment limits, check communication redundancies, and evaluate human decision-making under pressure.

This manual provides a comprehensive framework for the complete lifecycle of a professional mountain rescue exercise. It is divided into three distinct phases: **Phase I: Planning and Design**, **Phase II: Execution and Control**, and **Phase III: Documentation and Review**. Following this guide ensures that exercises yield actionable intelligence, improve team cohesion, and maintain safety standards in accordance with the highest operational norms.

## Phase I: Planning and Design

Planning is the most critical phase of an exercise. A poorly planned exercise is merely a risky hike; a well-planned exercise is a scientific assessment tool. This phase typically occurs 4–8 weeks before the scheduled date.

### 1.1 Defining the Exercise Objectives
The first step is to establish the "Purpose Statement." The command structure must decide what specific competencies need to be validated. Objectives should adhere to the SMART criteria (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound).

*   **Variable Types:**
    *   *Logistical:* Can our teams establish a base camp at an altitude of 2,500 meters within 4 hours?
    *   *Medical:* Can the team perform a surgical shunt of a pneumothorax in static wind conditions?
    *   *Command and Control:* Can the Incident Commander (IC) manage three-tiered asset allocation (Newtech, Avalanche, Multipurpose) without communication failure?
    *   *Communication:* Does the VHF radio network drop out in specific canyons? If so, what is the backup protocol?
    *   *Human Factors:* How does team fatigue affect decision-making after 6 hours of continuous operation?

*   **Complexity Levels:**
    *   *Level 1 (Basic):* Single casualty, good weather, single team.
    *   *Level 2 (Intermediate):* Multiple casualties, deteriorating weather, multi-team coordination.
    *   *Level 3 (Complex):* Mass casualty incident (MCI), aircraft incursion (helicopter), mixed terrain (river/ice/rock), and concurrent tasks.

### 2.1 Cost & Benefit Analysis and Scope
Once objectives are set, the scope must be defined to manage risk and budget.
*   **Scale:** How many "players" (rescuers), "hazards" (casualties/actors), and "observers" are required?
*   **Geography:** Select a location that mirrors the objective environment but allows for safe control. Avoid areas with active avalanche denominations or unstable rockfall zones unless specifically testing reaction to these hazards.
*   **Impact on Public:** If the exercise involves helicopters or large numbers of personnel, assess the impact on local public and tourism. Notification protocols must be in place to prevent panic if the public encounters the exercise.

### 2.2 The Safety Framework
Safety is paramount. An exercise that results in injury has failed its primary purpose. A formal Risk Assessment and Method Statement (RAMS) document is mandatory.

*   **Critical Control Measures (CCMs):**
    *   **Medical Cover:** Dedicated, non-participating medical staff must be on-site. They cannot play the role of rescuers; their job is to ensure rescuers do not get hurt.
    *   **Stop-Work Authority:** Every participant, from the IC down to the newest member, must have the explicit authority to stop the exercise if they perceive an unsafe condition.
    *   **Abort Criteria:** Pre-defined conditions under which the exercise is terminated immediately (e.g., lightning strike within 5km, severe injury to a participant, breakdown of comms).
    *   **Evacuation Routes:** For every location chosen, a 10-minute and a 30-minute evacuation route must be identified and communicated to all groups.

### 2.3 Roles and Responsibilities
Clear role allocation prevents confusion. The structure should mirror operational hierarchy.

*   **Exercise Director (ED):** The ultimate authority on the exercise. The ED ensures the exercise stays on track but does *not* participate in the rescue team.
*   **Movement Controllers (MCs):** "Super-observers" who maintain the timeline. They insert scenarios and check timing. They resemble the victim or the environment but do not engage in rescue tasks.
*   **Spectators/Observers:** Responsible for recording data without influencing the behavior of the rescuers. They must remain passive.
*   **Emergency Standby:** Police, Civil Defense, or local EMS kept on standby in case of real emergencies occurring during the exercise hours.

### 2.4 Scenario Design
The scenario is the narrative arc of the exercise. It should follow a logical progression of deterioration or complexity.

*   **The Narrative:** Start with a plausible incident (e.g., a hiker slips on a scree slope). Introduce variables:
    *   *T0 + 0 min:* Initial alarm.
    *   *T0 + 30 min:* Casualty is located but is conscious, bleeding internally, and hypothermic.
    *   *T0 + 90 min:* Weather turns. The team must make a decision: continue extraction or establish a bivouac?
    *   *T0 + 3 hours:* A second casualty is discovered. Resources are scarce.
    *   *Climax:* A medical emergency occurs (e.g., cardiac arrest) requiring resuscitation while managing environmental hazards.

*   **Injects:** These are unexpected changes introduced by the ED during the exercise. Examples: "The helicopter is grounded due to low visibility," or "The radio is dead." Injects test adaptability.

### 2.5 Administrative and Legal Preparations
*   **Permissions:** Obtain approvals from landowners, national parks, or forest services.
*   **Insurance:** Ensure all participants are covered under the rescue service’s liability insurance for the specific activity.
*   **Documentation Templates:** Prepare debriefing forms, scoring sheets, and recording checklists in advance.

## Phase II: Execution and Control (D-Day)

Execution is the kinetic phase. The goal is to create a realistic pressure environment while maintaining absolute safety. The atmosphere should be tense but controlled.

### 3.1 Pre-Exercise Briefing (T-2 Hours)
All personnel, including observers and emergency standbys, gather for a mandatory briefing.
1.  **Safety Review:** Review the RAMS. Discuss communication trees. Confirm abort codes.
2.  **Roles Clarification:** Who is playing the casualty? Who is the controller? Who is recording?
3.  **Ground Rules:**
    *   Do not shout "Stop" unless it is a genuine safety threat (use a specific code word like "Red Flag" for procedural stops vs. "Drop" for safety stops).
    *   Dress code: Casualties should wear appropriate clothing to simulate the scenario (including weather gear).
    *   Equipment check: Ensure all mandatory PPE (harnesses, helmets) is worn.
4.  **Timeline Walkthrough:** Walk through the scheduled events so everyone knows when major injects will occur.

### 3.2 Setup and Deployment
*   **Environment Setup:** Movement Controllers set up the "casualty" areas. If using helicopters, coordinate with air safety officers to establish Exclusion Zones.
*   **Observer Placement:** Observers move to vantage points. They must be positioned to see actions but remain invisible to the rescue teams to avoid the "Hawthorne Effect" (where people perform differently because they know they are being watched).
*   **Comms Check:** A full radio check of all channels. Establish a quiet channel for observers/MCs to discuss exercise progress without the rescue team hearing it.

### 3.3 The Exercise Run
The exercise begins at the scheduled start time (T=0).

*   **Task 1: Activation and Logon:** The scenario is activated (e.g., the "disaster victim" sends a distress signal). The rescue service logs onto the channel. Assure that the IC correctly identifies their role and establishes command authority.
*   **Task 2: Location and Access:** The primary team deploys. Observers note:
    *   Does the team carry the correct gear?
    *   Is the approach route sensible?
    *   Are they talking to each other effectively?
*   **Task 3: First Aid and Stabilization:** The team reaches the casualty. Here, technical medical skills are tested.
    *   *Observation Focus:* Airway management, bleeding control, packaging/spinal stabilization. Did they communicate the casualty’s status to Base?
