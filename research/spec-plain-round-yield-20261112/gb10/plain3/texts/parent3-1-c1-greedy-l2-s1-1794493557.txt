# The Comprehensive Manual for Mountain Rescue Exercises: Planning, Execution, and Documentation

## Introduction

Mountain rescue is a discipline that sits at the intersection of emergency medicine, wilderness survival, logistics, and high-stakes decision-making. Unlike urban emergency services, where response times are measured in minutes and infrastructure is robust, mountain rescue operates in environments where communication is spotty, terrain is hostile, and the margin for error is non-existent. Consequently, the competence of a mountain rescue team (MRT) is not merely a product of individual skill, but of rigorous, systematic, and repetitive training.

The exercise is the primary vehicle for this training. It is the bridge between theoretical knowledge and practical application. A well-planned exercise does not just test a team; it reveals the gaps in their preparation, tests their equipment under stress, and, most importantly, builds the cohesive muscle memory required to save lives in the dark, in the storm, or on a cliff face. This manual provides a detailed, end-to-end guide on how to plan, execute, and document a mountain rescue exercise. It is designed for team leaders, exercise controllers, and administrative staff responsible for maintaining operational readiness.

## Part I: The Philosophy and Objectives of Exercise

Before diving into logistics, one must understand the purpose of the exercise. An exercise is not a competition, nor is it a performance for spectators. It is a controlled failure environment. The goal is to fail safely so that the team does not fail catastrophically in a real incident.

### 1.1 Defining the Scope
Exercises range in complexity from "drills" to "full-scale simulations."
*   **Skill Drills:** Focused on a single technical skill (e.g., setting up a pulley system, splinting a fracture). These are low-stakes, high-repetition, and usually conducted in a training ground.
*   **Scenario Exercises:** Simulate a specific incident type (e.g., a hypothermic casualty in a crevasse). These involve multiple teams and require coordination.
*   **Full-Scale Exercises:** Involve external agencies (police, air ambulance, local authorities), use live actors (casualties), and simulate the entire chain of command from the initial 999/112 call to the handover of the patient.

### 1.2 The "Why" – Key Objectives
Every exercise must have defined objectives. Without them, the debrief becomes a vague discussion of feelings rather than a data-driven analysis of performance. Common objectives include:
*   **Technical Proficiency:** Can the team deploy a helicopter winch in under 10 minutes?
*   **Communication:** Can the team maintain clear, concise radio contact in high-wind conditions?
*   **Logistics:** Can the team sustain a casualty for 12 hours in a remote bivouac?
*   **Inter-Agency Coordination:** How effectively does the MRT interface with the police and air ambulance?
*   **Stress Management:** How do team members perform when the "casualty" is screaming, bleeding, and the weather is turning?

### 1.3 The Safety First Principle
The cardinal rule of any exercise is that no one gets hurt. This applies to the rescuers, the actors playing casualties, and the public. The safety of the exercise must always take precedence over the realism of the scenario. If a planned maneuver is too dangerous to execute safely, it must be modified or cancelled.

## Part II: The Planning Phase

Planning is where the exercise is won or lost. A poorly planned exercise is a waste of resources and a potential safety hazard. The planning phase should begin at least 4–6 weeks before the exercise date.

### 2.1 Forming the Planning Committee
A single individual should never plan a complex exercise alone. A Planning Committee (PC) should be formed, consisting of:
*   **Exercise Controller (EC):** The "God" of the exercise. They know the script, they control the timeline, and they have the authority to stop the exercise.
*   **Safety Officer (SO):** Independent of the EC. Their sole job is to monitor safety. They have veto power over any action.
*   **Logistics Coordinator:** Handles transport, food, equipment, and venue.
*   **Medical Liaison:** Ensures the medical scenarios are realistic and that real medical support is available for the actors.
*   **Public Relations (PR) Officer:** Manages media and public perception.

### 2.2 Selecting the Scenario
The scenario must be relevant to the local terrain and the team’s current weaknesses.
*   **Terrain Analysis:** Choose a location that represents the worst-case scenario for your area. If you are in a rocky alpine environment, do not practice in a flat meadow. Use cliffs, scree slopes, or dense forest.
*   **Weather Dependency:** Plan for the weather you expect, but have a contingency for the weather you hope for. If you plan a helicopter winch exercise, you need a clear day. If you plan a navigation exercise, you need fog.
*   **Casualty Profile:** Define the "patient." Are they conscious? Unconscious? Bleeding? Hypothermic? The medical condition dictates the rescue strategy. A conscious, stable patient requires a different approach than a cardiac arrest victim.

### 2.3 The Script and Timeline
The Exercise Controller must write a detailed script. This is not a novel; it is a technical document.
*   **The Trigger:** How does the incident start? A phone call? A radio transmission? A visual sighting?
*   **The Timeline:** Break the exercise down into 15-minute blocks.
    *   *T-0:* Incident occurs.
    *   *T+5:* Initial call received.
    *   *T+15:* Team mobilizes.
    *   *T+30:* Team arrives at scene.
    *   *T+45:* Casualty stabilized.
    *   *T+60:* Extraction begins.
*   **Injects:** These are unexpected events introduced by the EC to test adaptability. Examples: "The helicopter is delayed by 30 minutes due to weather," "The casualty’s condition deteriorates," or "A second casualty is discovered." Injects should be pre-planned but delivered at the right moment to maximize stress.

### 2.4 Risk Assessment and Method Statement
This is the legal and safety backbone of the exercise.
*   **Risk Assessment:** Identify every hazard. Slips, trips, falls, vehicle accidents, adverse weather, interaction with the public, and psychological stress. For each hazard, define the risk level (Low/Medium/High) and the mitigation strategy.
*   **Method Statement:** A step-by-step guide on how the exercise will be run. Who does what, where, and when. This document is signed off by the Safety Officer and the Team Leader.
*   **Permits and Permissions:** Secure permission from landowners, local authorities, and air traffic control. If you are using a helicopter, you need a flight plan. If you are on private land, you need a signed waiver.

### 2.5 Logistics and Resources
*   **Equipment Check:** All gear must be inspected and ready. This includes ropes, harnesses, first aid kits, radios, and vehicles.
*   **Transport:** How will the team get to the scene? How will the "casualty" be transported out?
*   **Sustenance:** Mountain rescue is physically demanding. Plan for hot food, hydration, and rest breaks for the team.
*   **Communication Plan:** Establish a dedicated radio channel for the exercise. Ensure all participants have the correct frequencies. Have a backup communication method (e.g., satellite phone or runner) in case of radio failure.

### 2.6 Briefing the Participants
One week before the exercise, all participants (rescuers, actors, observers) must be briefed.
*   **Rescuers:** They are told the general objective but *not* the specific details of the scenario. They must react to the situation as if it were real.
*   **Actors (Casualties):** They are briefed on their role. They must act realistically but safely. They are instructed on how to signal for help, how to simulate injuries, and, crucially, how to "break character" if they feel unsafe. A safe word (e.g., "Red Apple") should be established to stop the exercise immediately.
*   **Observers:** They are given a scoring sheet and instructions on what to watch for. They are not to interfere with the rescue.
