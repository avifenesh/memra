# The Comprehensive Manual for Mountain Rescue Exercises: Planning, Execution, and Documentation

## Introduction

Mountain rescue is a discipline that sits at the intersection of emergency medicine, wilderness survival, logistics, and high-stakes decision-making. Unlike urban emergency services, where response times are measured in minutes and infrastructure is robust, mountain rescue operates in environments where communication is spotty, terrain is hostile, and the margin for error is non-existent. Consequently, the difference between a successful rescue and a tragedy often lies not in the heroism of the moment, but in the rigor of the preparation.

This manual provides a comprehensive guide to the lifecycle of a mountain rescue exercise. It is designed for rescue team leaders, coordinators, and training officers. It covers the three critical phases of an exercise: **Planning** (the intellectual and logistical architecture), **Execution** (the operational reality), and **Documentation** (the analytical feedback loop). This document assumes a standard volunteer-based mountain rescue organization (MRO) structure, though principles apply to military and professional units as well.

## Part I: The Planning Phase

The planning phase is where the exercise is won or lost. A poorly planned exercise is merely a hike with a radio; a well-planned exercise is a controlled simulation of chaos that tests specific competencies.

### 1.1 Defining Objectives and Scope

Before selecting a location or a scenario, the planning committee must define the *why*. What is the purpose of this exercise?

*   **Skill Validation:** Are we testing a new piece of equipment (e.g., a new winch system or satellite communicator)?
*   **Team Integration:** Are we introducing new members to the team?
*   **Inter-Agency Coordination:** Are we practicing joint operations with the police, air ambulance, or local fire service?
*   **Specific Scenario Mastery:** Are we focusing on a specific type of incident, such as a hypothermic casualty in a crevasse, or a multi-casualty avalanche?

Once the objective is clear, the scope must be defined. Is this a tabletop exercise (TTE), a field exercise, or a full-scale simulation?
*   **Tabletop Exercises:** Low stress, high discussion. Ideal for testing command structures and decision-making without physical exertion.
*   **Field Exercises:** Moderate stress. Teams move to a location and perform tasks.
*   **Full-Scale Simulations:** High stress. Involves actors (casualties), realistic timelines, and often involves other agencies.

### 1.2 The Planning Committee and Roles

A single individual should never plan a complex exercise alone. A planning committee should be formed, consisting of:
*   **Exercise Director (ED):** The overall authority. They have the final say on safety and scope.
*   **Scenario Designer:** Responsible for the narrative, the "injection" of events, and the behavior of the simulated casualty.
*   **Safety Officer (SO):** An independent role. The SO has the authority to stop the exercise if safety standards are breached. They are not part of the rescue team.
*   **Logistics Coordinator:** Handles transport, food, water, and equipment checks.
*   **Control Room Manager:** Manages the "back office," radio traffic, and communication with the "public" (simulated).

### 1.3 Scenario Design: The Art of Realism

The scenario is the heart of the exercise. It must be realistic but manageable. A common mistake is creating a scenario that is physically impossible or medically improbable.

**Key Elements of a Scenario:**
1.  **The Incident:** What happened? (e.g., A hiker slipped on a scree slope and fractured their leg.)
2.  **The Environment:** Weather conditions, time of day, terrain difficulty.
3.  **The Casualty Profile:** Age, fitness level, medical history, and psychological state. A panicked, hypothermic casualty behaves very differently from a calm, fit one.
4.  **Complications (Injections):** These are events introduced during the exercise to test adaptability. Examples:
    *   "The weather is turning; visibility is dropping to zero."
    *   "The casualty’s condition is deteriorating; they are becoming confused."
    *   "The helicopter cannot land due to wind; you must perform a long carry."
    *   "Radio contact is lost with the control room."

**The "Twist" Factor:**
A good scenario includes a twist that forces the team to deviate from their initial plan. If the team plans to carry the casualty down, the twist might be that the casualty’s injury worsens, requiring a winch or a different extraction method. This tests the team’s ability to pivot under pressure.
