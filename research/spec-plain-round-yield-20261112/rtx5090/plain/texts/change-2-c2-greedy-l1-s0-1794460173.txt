# The Comprehensive Manual for Coastal Surveying: Planning, Execution, and Reporting

## Introduction

Coastal surveying is a specialized branch of geodesy and hydrography that deals with the precise measurement and mapping of coastal areas, including the shoreline, intertidal zones, nearshore waters, and the transition from land to sea. Unlike terrestrial surveying, which deals with static objects on solid ground, or deep-water hydrography, which focuses on open ocean depths, coastal surveying operates in a dynamic, high-risk environment where the boundary between land and water is constantly shifting.

The primary objectives of a coastal survey are threefold: to establish accurate geodetic control, to map the bathymetry (underwater topography) and topography (land topography) with high precision, and to identify hazards such as shoals, wrecks, and submerged obstructions. The data generated from these surveys is critical for maritime navigation, coastal engineering, environmental monitoring, and legal boundary demarcation.

This manual provides a detailed, step-by-step guide on how a professional coastal survey is planned, executed, and documented. It is designed for surveyors, project managers, and engineers involved in coastal projects.

## Part I: Pre-Survey Planning and Preparation

The success of a coastal survey is determined 80% before the vessel leaves the dock. Thorough planning mitigates risks, ensures data quality, and optimizes resource allocation.

### 1.1 Defining the Scope and Objectives

The first step is to define the "Why" and "What" of the survey. The scope must be clearly articulated in a Statement of Work (SOW). Key questions to answer include:

*   **Purpose:** Is this for navigation safety (hydrographic), coastal defense engineering (topographic/bathymetric), environmental impact assessment, or legal boundary determination?
*   **Accuracy Requirements:** What is the required vertical accuracy (e.g., ±0.15m for IHO Order 1) and horizontal accuracy (e.g., ±0.5m)?
*   **Area of Interest (AOI):** Define the boundaries using geographic coordinates. Include a buffer zone (typically 10–20% of the area) to account for tidal variations and vessel maneuvering space.
*   **Deliverables:** What format is required? (e.g., S-57 nautical charts, GeoTIFF rasters, CAD drawings, or raw point clouds).

### 2.2 Regulatory and Legal Compliance

Coastal waters are subject to strict international and national regulations. The surveyor must identify:

*   **International Hydrographic Organization (IHO) Standards:** Determine the required survey order (Order 1, 2, or 3) based on the intended use of the chart.
*   **National Regulations:** Check with local maritime authorities, environmental agencies, and landowners. Permits may be required for operating in protected marine areas, near ports, or within territorial waters.
*   **Environmental Constraints:** Identify no-go zones, such as marine sanctuaries, coral reefs, or nesting sites for protected species.

### 3.3 Data Collection and Pre-Survey Analysis

Before mobilizing equipment, the team must gather existing data to inform the survey design:

*   **Historical Charts:** Review existing nautical charts to identify known hazards, depth contours, and previous survey lines.
*   **Tidal Data:** Obtain tidal tables for the survey area. Understanding the tidal range is crucial for planning when to survey specific depths. For example, shallow areas may only be accessible during low tide, while deep channels may require high tide to avoid grounding.
*   **Meteorological and Oceanographic Data:** Analyze historical wind, wave, and current data. Coastal areas are often exposed to strong winds and currents. Knowing the prevailing conditions helps in scheduling the survey during calmer periods.
*   **Geodetic Control:** Identify existing geodetic control points (GNSS reference stations) in the vicinity. If none exist, a local control network must be established.

### 4.4 Equipment Selection and Calibration

The choice of equipment depends on the required accuracy and the environment.

*   **Positioning System:** A Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) is the standard. It provides centimeter-level horizontal and vertical accuracy. A base station or network RTK service is required.
*   **Depth Sounder:**
    *   *Single-Beam Echo Sounder (SBES):* Suitable for narrow channels or low-budget surveys. It measures depth along a single line.
    *   *Multibeam Echo Sounder (MBES):* The industry standard for coastal surveys. It uses an array of transducers to measure hundreds of depths simultaneously, creating a swath of data. This allows for faster coverage and higher resolution.
*   **Inertial Navigation System (INS):** Essential for MBES surveys. It measures the vessel’s roll, pitch, and yaw to correct for the movement of the boat, ensuring the soundings are accurately positioned.
*   **Tide Gauge:** A local tide gauge or a pressure sensor (barograph) must be deployed to record water levels in real-time. This is critical for converting soundings from "depth below water surface" to "depth below chart datum."
*   **Calibration:** All equipment must be calibrated before the survey.
    *   *Sound Velocity Profile (SVP):* Sound travels at different speeds in water depending on temperature, salinity, and pressure. An SVP cast must be taken daily to correct the echo sounder’s depth readings.
    *   *MBES Calibration:* The transducer’s offset (position relative to the GNSS antenna) and the INS alignment must be verified using a calibration run over a known flat area or a calibration tank.

### 5.5 Survey Design and Line Planning

The survey design is the blueprint for data collection.

*   **Line Spacing:** The distance between survey lines is determined by the MBES swath width and the required overlap. Typically, 20–30% overlap is recommended to ensure no gaps in coverage.
*   **Line Orientation:** Lines should generally run perpendicular to the shoreline or the main channel to maximize coverage efficiency.
*   **Tidal Windows:** Plan the survey lines so that shallow areas are surveyed at low tide and deep areas at high tide. This may require splitting the survey into multiple days or shifts.
*   **Safety Zones:** Mark no-go zones (e.g., near buoys, piers, or other traffic) on the planning software.

## Part II: Execution of the Coastal Survey

The execution phase involves mobilizing the team, deploying equipment, and collecting data in the field. Safety is the paramount concern.

### 6.1 Mobilization and Site Setup

*   **Vessel Selection:** Choose a vessel suitable for the conditions. For coastal surveys, a rigid inflatable boat (RIB) or a small survey catamaran is common. The vessel must be stable enough to keep the MBES transducer submerged and out of the propeller wash.
*   **Base Station Setup:** If using a local GNSS base station, set it up on stable, high ground with a clear view of the sky. The base station must be connected to the vessel via radio or cellular link for real-time corrections.
*   **Tide Gauge Deployment:** Install the tide gauge in a location representative of the survey area. It should be protected from waves but exposed to the water level. Record the gauge’s zero height relative to a known benchmark.
*   **Pre-Run Checks:**
    *   Check battery levels on all equipment.
    *   Verify GNSS connection and fix quality (PDOP < 2.0).
    *   Test the MBES and INS for proper data logging.
    *   Conduct a safety briefing with the crew, covering emergency procedures, man-overboard drills, and communication protocols.

### 7.2 Data Collection Procedures

*   **Sound Velocity Profile (SVP):** Take an SVP cast at the start of each day and whenever there is a significant change in water temperature or salinity. The SVP data is used to calculate the sound speed profile, which is applied to the depth sounder to correct for refraction errors.
*   **Survey Lines:**
    *   Follow the planned lines as closely as possible.
    *   Maintain a constant speed (typically 3–5 knots) to ensure consistent data quality.
    *   Keep the vessel centered on the line. Deviations can cause gaps or excessive overlap.
    *   Monitor the data in real-time. Look for anomalies such as sudden depth changes, which may indicate hazards or equipment errors.
*   **Tidal Corrections:** The tide gauge data is continuously recorded. At the end of the day, the tide data is processed to determine the water level at the time of each sounding. This is used to convert the raw depth to a common datum (e.g., Lowest Astronomical Tide - LAT).
*   **Quality Control (QC) in the Field:**
    *   Check for gaps in coverage. If a gap is detected, run a "fill-in" line immediately.
    *   Monitor the GNSS fix. If the fix is lost, stop the survey and wait for a re-fix. Do not continue collecting data without a valid position.
    *   Log any incidents, such as equipment failures, weather changes, or safety events
