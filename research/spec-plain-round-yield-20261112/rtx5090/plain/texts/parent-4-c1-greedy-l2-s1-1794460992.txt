# The Comprehensive Manual for Mountain Rescue Exercises: Planning, Execution, and Documentation

## Introduction

Mountain rescue is a discipline that sits at the intersection of emergency medicine, wilderness survival, logistics, and high-stakes decision-making. Unlike urban emergency services, where response times are measured in minutes and infrastructure is robust, mountain rescue operates in environments where communication is spotty, terrain is hostile, and the margin for error is non-existent. Consequently, the difference between a successful rescue and a tragedy often lies not in the heroism of the moment, but in the rigor of the preparation.

This manual provides a comprehensive guide to the lifecycle of a mountain rescue exercise. It is designed for rescue team leaders, coordinators, and training officers. It covers the three critical phases of an exercise: **Planning** (the intellectual and logistical architecture), **Execution** (the operational reality), and **Documentation** (the analytical feedback loop). This document assumes a standard volunteer-based mountain rescue organization (MRO) structure, though principles apply to military and professional units as well.

## Part I: The Planning Phase

The planning phase is where the exercise is won or lost. A poorly planned exercise is merely a hike with a radio; a well-planned exercise is a controlled simulation of chaos that tests specific competencies.

### 1.1 Defining Objectives and Scope

Before selecting a location or a scenario, the planning committee must define the *why*. What is the purpose of this exercise?

*   **Skill Validation:** Are we testing a new piece of equipment (e.g., a new winch system or satellite communicator)?
*   **Team Integration:** Are we introducing new members to the team?
*   **Inter-Agency Coordination:** Are we practicing joint operations with the police, air ambulance, or local fire service?
*   **Specific Scenario Mastery:** Are we focusing on a specific type of incident, such as a hypothermic casualty in a crevasse, or a multi-casualty avalanche?

Once the objective is clear, the scope must be defined. Is this a tabletop exercise (TTE), a field exercise, or a full-scale simulation?
*   **Tabletop Exercises:** Low stress, high discussion. Ideal for testing command structures and decision-making without physical exertion.
*   **Field Exercises:** Moderate stress. Teams move to a location and perform tasks.
*   **Full-Scale Simulations:** High stress. Involves actors (casualties), realistic timelines, and often involves other agencies.

### 1.2 The Planning Committee and Roles

A single individual should never plan a complex exercise alone. A planning committee should be formed, consisting of:
*   **Exercise Director (ED):** The overall authority. They have the final say on safety and scope.
*   **Scenario Designer:** Responsible for the narrative, the "injection" of events, and the behavior of the simulated casualty.
*   **Safety Officer (SO):** An independent role. The SO has the authority to stop the exercise if safety standards are breached. They are not part of the rescue team.
*   **Logistics Coordinator:** Handles transport, food, water, and equipment checks.
*   **Control Room Manager:** Manages the "back office," radio traffic, and communication with the "public" (simulated).

### 1.3 Scenario Design: The Art of Realism

The scenario is the heart of the exercise. It must be realistic but manageable. A common mistake is creating a scenario that is physically impossible or medically improbable.

**Key Elements of a Scenario:**
1.  **The Incident:** What happened? (e.g., A hiker slipped on a scree slope and fractured their leg.)
2.  **The Environment:** Weather conditions, time of day, terrain difficulty.
3.  **The Casualty Profile:** Age, fitness level, medical history, and psychological state. A panicked, hypothermic casualty behaves very differently from a calm, fit one.
4.  **Complications (Injections):** These are events introduced during the exercise to test adaptability. Examples:
    *   "The weather is turning; visibility is dropping to zero."
    *   "The casualty’s condition is deteriorating; they are becoming confused."
    *   "The helicopter cannot land due to wind; you must perform a long carry."
    *   "Radio contact is lost with the control room."

**The "Twist" Factor:**
A good scenario includes a twist that forces the team to deviate from their initial plan. If the team plans to carry the casualty down, the twist might be that the casualty’s injury worsens, requiring a winch or a different extraction method. This tests the team’s ability to pivot under pressure.

### 1.4 Logistics and Resource Management

Mountain rescue is resource-intensive. The planning phase must account for:
*   **Equipment Audit:** Check all ropes, harnesses, winches, first aid kits, and communication devices. Ensure batteries are charged and spares are available.
*   **Transport:** How will the team get to the start point? How will the "casualty" be transported to the "hospital" (usually a designated safe zone)?
*   **Sustenance:** Mountain rescue is physically demanding. Plan for hot food, hydration, and rest breaks. Dehydration and hypoglycemia impair decision-making.
*   **Medical Support:** A qualified medical professional (doctor or paramedic) should be present on-site, separate from the rescue team, to monitor the "casualty" (actor) and ensure no real harm occurs.

### 1.5 Risk Assessment and Safety Protocols

This is the most critical document in the planning phase. A formal Risk Assessment (RA) must be completed for every aspect of the exercise.

*   **Terrain Risks:** Slips, falls, rockfall, crevasses.
*   **Weather Risks:** Hypothermia, lightning, whiteout conditions.
*   **Operational Risks:** Rope failure, winch malfunction, communication breakdown.
*   **Psychological Risks:** Stress on participants, especially new members.

**Mitigation Strategies:**
*   **Buddy System:** No one works alone.
*   **Abort Criteria:** Pre-defined conditions under which the exercise is stopped (e.g., lightning within 10 miles, casualty actor reports pain).
*   **Safety Briefing:** All participants must attend a briefing before the exercise starts. They must understand the "rules of the game" (e.g., "If you hear the whistle, stop everything and report to the Safety Officer").

### 1.6 Communication Plan

In the field, communication is the lifeline. The plan must specify:
*   **Radio Channels:** Primary, secondary, and emergency channels.
*   **Call Signs:** Unique identifiers for each team member and unit.
*   **Protocol:** Standardized phrases (e.g., "Copy," "Roger," "Break," "Over").
*   **Backup:** What happens if the radio fails? (e.g., Visual signals, pre-arranged meeting points, satellite phone).

## Part II: The Execution Phase

The execution phase is where the plan meets reality. It is dynamic, stressful, and unpredictable. The goal is to observe how the team performs under pressure, not to force them to succeed.

### 2.1 Pre-Exercise Briefing

On the day of the exercise, a final briefing is held. This is not a lecture; it is a confirmation of understanding.
*   **Roles:** Reiterate who is doing what.
*   **Safety:** Review the abort criteria and emergency procedures.
*   **Scenario Hints:** Do *not* give away the plot. Only provide the initial information the team would receive in a real incident (e.g., "A male hiker is reported missing near the North Ridge. Last seen at 14:00.").
*   **Casualty Actor Briefing:** The actor must be briefed separately. They must know their medical history, their emotional state, and how to react to treatment. They must also know the safety signals (e.g., a specific whistle blast means "stop acting and check for real injury").

### 2.2 The "Control Room"

The Control Room is the nerve center of the exercise. It is usually located at a base camp or a nearby village. It simulates the dispatch center or the incident command post.

**Functions of the Control Room:**
1.  **Information Injection:** They release information to the field teams at specific times. (e.g., "Police report the casualty was last seen wearing a red jacket.")
2.  **Monitoring:** They listen to all radio traffic. They do not intervene unless the team is in danger or the scenario is breaking down.
3.  **Timing:** They keep a strict log of when actions occur. This is crucial for the post-exercise analysis.
4.  **Liaison:** If other agencies are involved, the Control Room manages their entry into the scenario.

**The "God View":**
The Control Room has a "god view" of the entire exercise. They know where the casualty is, what the weather is, and what the next injection is. The field teams do not. This asymmetry of information is what creates the tension.

### 2.3 Field Operations: The Rescue Team

The rescue team operates under the assumption that this is a real incident. They must:
*   **Assess:** Evaluate the situation, the casualty, and the environment.
*   **Plan:** Develop a rescue plan.
*   **Execute:** Carry out the plan.
*   **Adapt:** Change the plan when conditions change.

**Key Observations for the Safety Officer and Observers:**
*   **Communication:** Is the radio traffic clear, concise, and calm? Are they using standard terminology?
*   **Team Dynamics:** Is there a clear leader? Are roles understood? Is there conflict?
*   **Technical Skills:** Are knots tied correctly? Is the winch rigged safely? Is the casualty secured properly?
*   **Decision Making:** Are they making safe decisions? Are they rushing? Are they hesitating?
*   **Self-Care:** Are they checking their own physical and mental state?

### 2.4 Managing the "Casualty"

The casualty actor is a critical component. They must be realistic but safe.
*   **Medical Simulation:** The actor should wear a "sim-kit" (e.g., a padded leg to simulate a fracture, or a cold suit to simulate hypothermia). They should not actually be injured.
*   **Behavioral Cues:** The actor should follow a script. For example, if the team performs a splint, the actor should say, "That helps, but I’m still very cold." If the team is slow, the actor should become more agitated or confused.
*   **Safety Check:** The Safety Officer must check on the actor regularly. If the actor is genuinely cold or in pain, the exercise must be paused.
