# The Comprehensive Manual for Planning, Executing, and Documenting a Mountain Rescue Exercise

## Introduction

Mountain rescue operations are distinct from standard urban emergency services due to the extreme variability of the environment, the isolation of potential incidents, and the reliance on specialized gear and visceral decision-making under pressure. While the ultimate goal of any rescue service is to save lives in actual emergencies, the engine that drives operational excellence is the exercise (or "training drill").

This manual provides a rigorous, step-by-step guide for developing a comprehensive mountain rescue exercise. It covers the three distinct phases of the lifecycle: **Planning** (the design and preparation of a realistic scenario), **Execution** (the dynamic conduct of the event), and **Documentation** (the critical analysis and reporting that translates experience into permanent organizational improvement). A poorly planned exercise is a waste of resources and potentially dangerous; a well-planned exercise is the single most effective tool for testing equipment, validating tactics, and bonding teams. This document assumes the reader is familiar with the basics of rope work, cartography, and wilderness first aid, focusing instead on the *management* and *architecture* of the exercise itself.

## Part I: Planning Phase

The success of an exercise is determined almost entirely before the first participant puts on a helmet. The planning phase is not merely about choosing a day on the calendar; it is about defining a purpose, managing risk, and constructing a narrative that challenges the team’s specific weak points.

### 1.1 Defining Objectives and Scope

The first question any planning committee must answer is: *Why are we doing this?* "Because it’s nice to go out into the hills" is not a strategic objective. Objectives must be SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound).

**Strategic Objectives** might include:
*   Testing the effectiveness of a new static line crossing protocol.
*   Evaluating the communication reliability of the new satellite tracking devices in canopy-heavy terrain.
*   Inter-agency cooperation, specifically focusing on the handover between ground ambulances and air ambulances.
*   Multi-casualty triage in a confined, difficult-access environment (e.g., a narrow gully).

**Tactical Objectives** involve specific skills:
*   Planning and executing a fixed line rappel from a 60-meter cliff face.
*   Converging search teams using triangulation.
*   Medical stabilization of a hypothermic patient in wet conditions.

**Scope Definition:**
Once objectives are set, define the scope. Is this a narrative exercise (a role-play scenario where injuries are fictitious)? Or is it a practical skills day (e.g., everyone drills rope anchor setup)? For a high-value "full-scare" exercise, narrative is preferred. The scenario must be "plausible but exaggerated." It should be something that *could* happen, but rarely does, forcing the team to operate at peak performance without the stressor of actual human suffering involved in the decision-making process.

### 2. Location Reconnaissance and Risk Assessment

You cannot plan an exercise for a venue you have not physically walked. The "blank sheet of paper" approach is negligent.

**Site Selection Criteria:**
*   **Accessibility:** Can the "Injured Person Unit" (the actors and their medical support) get there safely and quickly? If the team has to haul a heavy stretcher up a steep cliff just to start the exercise, the tactical value is diminished.
*   **Communication:** Before committing, test the radio channels. If the summit has zero coverage and the team consented to use compressed data radios, the scenario must reflect that constraint.
*   **Weather Predictability:** Mountain weather is a variable. Choose a location where you can mitigate weather risks (e.g., staying in valleys if there is a risk of lightning) or design the exercise to be weather-agnostic (e.g., a tight rope crossing inside a sheltered cave system).

**Risk Assessment (RA):**
This is the legal and moral backbone of the exercise. In a real rescue, the risk is to the victim. In an exercise, the risk is to the *participants* (team members and actors).
*   **Fallow Ladder:** Use a Fallow Ladder or equivalent risk matrix. Ask: *What could go wrong?*
    *   *Scenario:* A 40-foot harrier rope rappel.
    *   *Risk:* A team member slips during the rappel.
    *   *Control Measure:* A dedicated "safety anchor" spotter who holds the brake bar and is not part of the participating rope team.
    *   *Risk:* Hypothermia for actors playing long-duration victims.
    *   *Control Measure:* Actors must wear hidden insulation under costumes. "Check-in" protocols every 30 minutes.
*   **Medical Safety Protocol:** Every actor victim must have a pre-agreed "out" signal. If an actor says "Code Red," all rescue efforts stop immediately, and they are treated as a real casualty. The medical team (not the rescue team) controls the care of the actors.

### 3.1 The Narrative Script

A realistic exercise requires a script, but not a rigid one. It should be a "living document" that the Exercise Controller (EXCON) manipulates based on real-time team performance.

**Plot Points:**
1.  **The Call:** Define the initial information. Is it an amateur radio call? A drone ping? A vague shout from a distance? *Challenge:* Ambiguous information.
2.  **The Deployment:** How do the teams get to the scene? Hike in from the valley floor? Helicopter insertion? *Challenge:* Weather turns, forcing a change in transport mode mid-exercise.
3.  **The Scene Assessment:** What do they find? The environment should be set up physically.
    *   If the scenario is a hiker caught in a crevasse, the rope setup must be in place.
    *   If the scenario is a multi-casualty avalanche, the "victims" must be arranged to require triage.
4.  **The Complication:** This is the heart of the exercise.
    *   *Example:* The primary extraction line is compromised by a gear failure (simulated by EXCON instructing the actor to stop climbing so the line is "stuck").
    *   *Example:* A second casualty is found during the search.
    *   *Example:* Night falls. The team must transition to night operations with lighting gear.

**Contingency Triggers:**
The EXCON must decide when to escalate or de-escalate.
*   *Trigger:* If the team is moving too slowly, EXCON may create "urgency" (e.g., "The victim’s body temp is dropping").
*   *Trigger:* If the team is struggling with coordination, EXCON may improve the scenario (e.g., "A local walker has passed by and heard the victim," giving more precise location data).

### 3.2 Logistics and Resourcing

**The Team Breakdown:**
*   **Response Teams:** The actual rescue team members undergoing testing. They should not know the specific scenario details, only the general type of incident (e.g., "multi-casualty alpine emergency").
*   **Exercise Control (EXCON):** The "Gods" of the exercise. They know the script, they manage the actors, and they inject events. They must remain impartial.
*   **Observers/Evaluators:** Distinct from EXCON. They do not interfere; they watch, measure times, note errors, and evaluate against the specific objectives.
*   **Actors (Victims):** Professional or experienced volunteers role-playing the injured. They must be briefed extensively. Their credibility is vital. If the "victim" laughs, the exercise breaks. They must act the part: breathing patterns, pain responses, confusion, hypothermia symptoms (shivering, slurred speech).
*   **Medical Safety Team:** Dedicated personnel ensuring the safety of actors and participants. They are not part of the rescue narrative.

**Equipment Checks:**
All equipment used in the exercise must be inspected. Even if the team uses their daily kit, it must be fit for purpose. If you are testing a new brand of pulley, ensure you send enough units (e.g., 3 spares) in case one fails, as "gear failure" is a valid test, but "out of gear" is a logistical failure.

**Communication Plan:**
Establish a private radio channel for EXCON, Observers, and Medical. The Rescue Teams should use their standard operational channels. There must be no "cheating" (e.g., EXCON accidentally revealing a plot point on the rescue channel).
