# The Comprehensive Manual for Mountain Rescue Exercises: Planning, Execution, and Documentation

## Introduction

Mountain rescue is a discipline that sits at the intersection of emergency medicine, wilderness survival, logistics, and high-stakes decision-making. Unlike urban emergency services, where response times are measured in minutes and infrastructure is robust, mountain rescue operates in environments where communication is spotty, terrain is hostile, and the margin for error is non-existent. Consequently, the competence of a mountain rescue team (MRT) is not merely a product of individual skill, but of rigorous, systematic, and repetitive training.

The exercise is the primary vehicle for this training. It is the bridge between theoretical knowledge and practical application. A well-planned exercise does not just test a team; it reveals the gaps in their preparation, tests their equipment under stress, and, most importantly, builds the cohesive muscle memory required to save lives in the dark, in the storm, or on a cliff face. This manual provides a detailed, end-to-end guide on how to plan, execute, and document a mountain rescue exercise. It is designed for team leaders, exercise controllers, and administrative staff responsible for maintaining operational readiness.

## Part I: The Philosophy and Objectives of Exercise

Before diving into logistics, one must understand the purpose of the exercise. An exercise is not a competition, nor is it a performance for spectators. It is a controlled failure environment. The goal is to fail safely so that the team does not fail catastrophically in a real incident.

### 1.1 Defining the Scope
Exercises range in complexity from "drills" to "full-scale simulations."
*   **Skill Drills:** Focused on a single technical skill (e.g., setting up a pulley system, splinting a fracture). These are low-stakes, high-repetition, and usually conducted in a training ground.
*   **Scenario Exercises:** Simulate a specific incident type (e.g., a hypothermic casualty in a crevasse). These involve multiple teams and require coordination.
*   **Full-Scale Exercises:** Involve external agencies (police, air ambulance, local authorities), use live actors (casualties), and simulate the entire chain of command from the initial 999/112 call to the handover of the patient.

### 1.2 The "Why" – Key Objectives
Every exercise must have defined objectives. Without them, the debrief becomes a vague discussion of feelings rather than a data-driven analysis of performance. Common objectives include:
*   **Technical Proficiency:** Can the team deploy a helicopter winch in under 10 minutes?
*   **Communication:** Can the team maintain clear, concise radio contact in high-wind conditions?
*   **Logistics:** Can the team sustain a casualty for 12 hours in a remote bivouac?
*   **Inter-Agency Coordination:** How effectively does the MRT interface with the police and air ambulance?
*   **Stress Management:** How do team members perform when the "casualty" is screaming, bleeding, and the weather is turning?

### 1.3 The Safety First Principle
The cardinal rule of any exercise is that no one gets hurt. This applies to the rescuers, the actors playing casualties, and the public. The safety of the exercise must always take precedence over the realism of the scenario. If a planned maneuver is too dangerous to execute safely, it must be modified or cancelled.
