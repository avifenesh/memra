# The Complete Manual for Coastal Surveying: Planning, Execution, and Reporting

## Introduction

Coastal surveying is a specialized discipline within the broader field of geospatial science and surveying that focuses on the detailed measurement, mapping, and monitoring of coastal zones. The coastal environment represents a dynamic interface between land and sea, characterized by constant change driven by waves, tides, currents, storms, and long-term processes such as sea-level rise and erosion. Because of this dynamism, coastal surveys must be conducted with precision, care, and a thorough understanding of the physical processes at play. This manual provides a comprehensive guide to the entire lifecycle of a coastal survey, from initial planning through field execution to the final written report. It is intended for surveyors, engineers, environmental scientists, students, and anyone involved in coastal management or research.

The coastal zone is one of the most important and most vulnerable environments on Earth. It hosts a significant proportion of the world's population, critical infrastructure, rich biodiversity, and economic activities ranging from fishing to tourism to shipping. Understanding the physical characteristics of the coast—its topography, bathymetry, sediment composition, vegetation distribution, and the forces that shape it—is essential for a wide range of applications. These include coastal engineering projects such as the construction of seawalls and harbors, environmental impact assessments, beach management and nourishment programs, flood risk modeling, habitat conservation, and the development of coastal zoning regulations.

A well-executed coastal survey provides the foundational data upon which all of these activities depend. Poor-quality data can lead to costly engineering failures, inaccurate environmental assessments, and misguided policy decisions. Therefore, the quality of a coastal survey depends not only on the sophistication of the equipment used but also on the rigor of the planning, the skill of the field team, and the clarity and completeness of the final report. This manual addresses each of these stages in detail.

## Part 1: Planning the Coastal Survey

### 1.1 Defining the Objectives

The very first step in any coastal survey is to define clearly what the survey is intended to achieve. The objectives will determine nearly every subsequent decision, including the choice of methods, the level of precision required, the extent of the study area, and the budget and timeline. Common objectives for coastal surveys include:

- **Mapping the coastal topography and bathymetry** to create detailed charts or digital elevation models of both the landward and seaward portions of the coast.
- **Monitoring coastal erosion and accretion** over time by conducting repeated surveys at the same locations.
- **Supporting engineering design** for structures such as breakwaters, jetties, piers, seawalls, and coastal defenses.
- **Assessing habitat distribution** such as salt marshes, dunes, coral reefs, mangroves, or seagrass beds.
- **Evaluating flood and inundation risk** in preparation for climate adaptation or emergency planning.
- **Supporting marine spatial planning** and the designation of protected areas.

The survey objectives should be written down explicitly and should be agreed upon by all stakeholders, including the client, the surveying team, and any regulatory bodies that may be involved. Ambiguous or poorly defined objectives are one of the most common sources of failure in surveying projects.

### 1.2 Desk Study and Literature Review

Before setting out into the field, a thorough desk study should be conducted. This involves gathering and reviewing all existing information about the survey area. Relevant sources of information include:

- **Existing maps and charts**, including nautical charts produced by hydrographic offices, topographic maps from national mapping agencies, and any previous surveys conducted in the area.
- **Aerial photographs and satellite imagery**, which can provide historical context and reveal changes in the coastline over time.
- **Meteorological and oceanographic data**, including records of tides, waves, currents, wind patterns, and storm history.
- **Geological and sedimentological data**, which describe the composition of the coastal materials.
- **Regulatory frameworks and legal requirements**, including any permits, environmental protections, or data standards that must be adhered to.

This desk study helps the surveyor understand the characteristics of the study area, identify potential hazards, and avoid duplicating work that has already been done. It also helps in the identification of any sensitive environmental areas that may require special handling.

### 1.3 Understanding the Coastal Environment

A successful coastal survey requires a genuine understanding of the physical processes that shape the coast. The surveyor should consider the following factors:

- **Tides:** The tidal regime of the area determines the range between high and low water, which affects when and how the survey can be conducted. Many coastal surveys must be timed to coincide with specific tidal conditions to capture the intertidal zone accurately.
- **Waves:** Wave action affects the morphology of the coast and can create hazardous working conditions. Wave climate data helps in assessing the feasibility of different survey methods.
- **Currents:** Coastal currents can affect the accuracy of certain measurement techniques, particularly those involving waterborne platforms.
- **Sediment type:** The nature of the sediments—whether sand, mud, gravel, or bedrock—affects the choice of equipment and the methods used for sampling.
- **Vegetation:** Coastal vegetation can complicate surveying on land but is also often an object of interest itself.
- **Human activity:** The presence of shipping, recreation, fishing, and other activities must be considered for both safety and data quality.

### 1.4 Determining the Extent and Resolution of the Survey

The surveyor must decide on the spatial extent of the survey and the resolution at which data will be collected. The extent defines the geographic boundaries of the study area, while the resolution defines the level of detail. Higher resolution data captures more detail but requires more time and resources. The appropriate resolution depends on the objectives of the survey. For example, a survey intended to support detailed engineering design will require higher resolution than one intended to provide a general overview of a large coastal region.

### 1.5 Selecting Appropriate Methods and Equipment

Coastal surveys can be conducted using a variety of methods, each with its own advantages and limitations. The selection of methods depends on the objectives, the environment, the required precision, and the budget. The main categories of coastal survey methods are described below.

**Land-based methods:**

- **Total stations:** These are electronic instruments that measure angles and distances to reflectors. They are highly accurate for measuring specific points and are useful for surveying accessible terrain.
- **Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS):** Modern GNSS receivers, including those that use GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou constellations, can provide highly accurate position data. Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) GNSS can achieve centimeter-level accuracy in real time and is widely used in coastal surveying.
- **Leveling:** Precise leveling using a dumpy level or digital level and staff can measure elevation differences with high accuracy and is often used to establish vertical control tied to a known datum.

**Airborne methods:**

- **Airborne LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging):** LiDAR systems mounted on aircraft or drones emit laser pulses that measure the distance to the surface. Airborne LiDAR can rapidly cover large areas and is particularly useful for creating digital elevation models. Coastal LiDAR, sometimes called beach profiling LiDAR, is specifically designed to measure the topography of the beach and dunes.
- **Airborne photography and photogrammetry:** Aerial photographs can be processed using photogrammetric techniques to extract topographic information.

**Umbcoastal and waterborne methods:**

- **Single-beam and multi-beam echo sounders:** These instruments measure water depth by emitting sound pulses and measuring the time taken for the echo to return. Multi-beam echo sounders cover a wide swath of the seafloor and are highly efficient for bathymetric surveying.
- **Remotely Operated Vehicles (ROVs) and Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs):** These underwater platforms can be equipped with sensors to map the seafloor and inspect structures.
- **Unmanned Surface Vehicles (USVs):** Sometimes called "boats without a crew," these can carry sonar and other sensors across the water surface.

**Ground-based remote sensing:**

- **Handheld or tripod-mounted LiDAR:** Portable LiDAR systems can be used to scan coastal cliffs, dunes, and other features.
- **Drone-based (UAV) LiDAR and photography:** Drones have become increasingly popular for coastal surveying because they are flexible, relatively inexpensive, and can access difficult terrain. Drone-based systems can carry cameras and LiDAR sensors to produce high-resolution imagery and 3D models.

The choice of methods may also involve a combination of techniques, as no single method can capture all aspects of a complex coastal environment. For example, a comprehensive coastal survey might combine airborne LiDAR for the subaerial topography, GNSS for ground control, and multi-beam sonar for the bathymetry, with the two datasets merged to create a continuous model of the coast from the dune crest to the deeper seafloor.

### 1.6 Establishing Control and Datum

Accurate surveying requires a framework of control points and a well-defined datum. Control points are locations of known position that serve as reference points for the measurements. The datum is the reference against which elevations and depths are measured. For coastal work, it is common to use a vertical datum based on sea level, such as the Mean Sea Level (MSL) or a specific tidal datum such as the Highest Astronomical Tide (HAT) or the Lowest Astronomical Tide (LAT). The horizontal control is typically referenced to a standard coordinate system such as the World Geodetic System (WGS84) or a national grid system.

The selection and establishment of control is critical because errors in the control framework propagate through the entire survey. Control points should be established, documented, and protected throughout the survey. In coastal environments, control points may be subject to movement due to erosion, construction, or other processes, so they should be checked periodically.

### 1.7 Risk Assessment and Safety Planning

Coastal surveying can involve significant hazards, including working near water, on unstable ground, in remote locations, and in exposure to weather. A thorough risk assessment must be conducted before any fieldwork begins. This should identify potential hazards such as drowning, tidal stranding, slippery surfaces, marine life, traffic, and remote location risks. A safety plan should be developed that includes procedures for emergencies, communication protocols, personal protective equipment, and training requirements. The safety of the field team must always be the top priority.

### 1.8 Budgeting and Scheduling

A realistic budget and schedule should be prepared, taking into account the cost of equipment, personnel, travel, data processing, and any permits or fees. The schedule should account for the time required for planning, fieldwork, data processing, and reporting, as well as for contingencies due to weather and other delays. Coastal work is particularly susceptible to weather-related delays, so it is prudent to build in buffer time.

## Part 2: Executing the Coastal Survey

### 2.1 Field Preparation

Before the fieldwork begins, all equipment should be checked, calibrated, and tested. This includes verifying that GNSS receivers, total stations, echo sounders, drones, and sensors are functioning correctly and that they have been calibrated according to the manufacturer's specifications. Batteries, storage media, and spare parts should be packed. Field data sheets or electronic data collection systems should be prepared. The team should review the survey plan, assign roles, and confirm that everyone understands their tasks and the safety procedures.

### 2.2 Establishing Control in the Field

Once in the field, the control framework must be established or verified. This may involve setting out new control points, or occupying and observing existing control points. For GNSS-based surveys, a reference station must be established at a location with a clear view of the sky and a known position. Long-base or real-time kinematic solutions should be used to ensure accuracy. For total station and leveling work, instrument setup positions must be chosen carefully to ensure line of sight and stability.

It is important to record all control data meticulously, including the coordinates, elevations, and the conditions under which measurements were taken. Any anomalies or discrepancies should be investigated immediately rather than being left until later.

### 2.3 Conducting the Measurements

The actual data collection phase should follow the survey plan closely while remaining flexible enough to respond to conditions encountered in the field. Different components of the survey may be conducted in a specific sequence. For example, the subaerial topography might be surveyed first, followed by the intertidal and subtidal zones as the tide recedes or advances.

When using GNSS, the surveyor should ensure adequate satellite geometry, appropriate number of epochs for averaging, and correct handling of the antenna. When using echo sounders, the speed of sound in the water must be accounted for, and corrections for the vessel's heave, pitch, and roll must be applied. The sound velocity profile of the water should be measured if significant variations are expected, as these can affect the accuracy of the soundings.

For drone-based surveys, flight plans should be programmed to ensure adequate overlap between images for photogrammetry or complete coverage for LiDAR. The flight should be monitored, and any issues such as loss of signal or unexpected obstacles should be addressed immediately.

During data collection, it is essential to maintain detailed field notes. These notes should record the conditions under which the data was collected, including weather, tidal state, time of day, and any observations that might be relevant to the interpretation of the data. Field notes also serve as a valuable record in the event of questions or disputes later on.
